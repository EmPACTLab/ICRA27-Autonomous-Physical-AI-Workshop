01 / The workshop

From robots that are told what to do to robots that work out what to do.

Physical AI has advanced quickly. Generalist robot policies can handle unfamiliar objects, vision–language–action models can turn requests into motor commands, and learned world models can predict plausible futures. Yet almost all of this progress assumes that somebody has already supplied the task.

In a home, hospital ward, or factory, much of what needs doing is never said. A spill on a walkway may call for help; a chair pushed out of place probably does not. This workshop asks how a robot can perceive a situation, infer what is needed and by whom, then decide whether to act, ask, wait, or leave things alone.

01 —

Discover the task

Recognize actionable situations rather than treating every novelty as a goal. Connect environmental changes, human cues, affordances, foundation models, and world models.

02 —

Decide responsibly

Reason about uncertainty, authority, permission, safety, and whether intervention is appropriate before a self-proposed action begins.

03 —

Verify the outcome

Ground autonomous initiative in physical execution and measurable outcomes, including the value of correct non-action and abstention.

Highlight of uniqueness. Unlike workshops centered on interaction-design outcomes or a particular reasoning architecture, this workshop focuses on the fundamental challenge of Physical AI: enabling robots to recognize what needs to be done, determine whether intervention is appropriate, and initiate safe physical actions without explicit instructions.
02 / Research questions

A shared formulation for autonomous initiative.

The workshop connects human-originated and environment-originated needs under one sequence: observe → discover candidate goals → decide to act, ask, observe, or abstain → execute → verify.

RQ1

What needs attention?

What signals distinguish an actionable state from a benign anomaly or an ordinary change in the environment?

RQ2

What could change?

How can foundation models, world models, affordances, and planning infer and rank candidate goals?

RQ3

What do people or situations require?

How can a physical AI-driven robot infer needs, preferences, and social constraints—and how sure must it be?

RQ4

When should the robot act?

How should uncertainty, permission, and safety shape action, and how can the robot verify and explain the outcome?

03 / Response to feedback

A focused, complementary agenda.

The proposal responds to the committee’s encouragement by clarifying its scientific scope, its distinction from proposals #160 and #201, its active-participation format, and the organizing team’s experience.

Comment 1 · Topic

Well-identified questions

We thank the committee for recognizing the relevance of the research questions. The revised proposal makes the central problem explicit: how robots discover and initiate appropriate physical action from unspoken human needs or unattended environmental conditions, without an assigned task.

Comment 2 · Distinction

Different questions and targets

Our workshop is centered on Physical AI and the robot’s decision to propose and initiate a physical task that no one has specified. It is method-agnostic and evaluates task discovery, intervention appropriateness, permission, safety, and verified outcomes.

Comment 3 · Expertise

A strengthened team

The organizers have run workshops at major robotics, vision, and AI venues and bring complementary expertise in embodied perception, autonomous reasoning, robot learning, multimodal AI, planning, and physical action.

Participation · Format

Designed for interaction

The half-day combines four invited talks, five contributed oral presentations, a poster and demonstration break, a hands-on scenario tutorial, and a panel that uses participant-generated agreements and disagreements.

07 / Call for contributions

Bring an early result, a benchmark, or a difficult question.

The public CFP welcomes non-archival submissions that help the community reason about self-initiated robot action.

What to submit

Two- to four-page papers, position papers, benchmarks and datasets, early results, negative results, or demonstration proposals.

How it will be reviewed

At least two conflict-aware reviews per submission, considering relevance, clarity, quality of evidence, safety considerations, and value for discussion.

What will be selected

Up to twelve submissions for poster or demonstration presentation; five will also be selected for oral presentation. Awards will recognize the best paper and best demonstration.

Who should participate

Researchers and practitioners in robotics, computer vision, machine learning, HRI, AI safety, and embodied AI—from academia and industry.

08 / Engagement and inclusion

Participation is part of the research design.

The workshop is built to keep participants actively involved throughout the morning and to turn discussion into useful, shared outputs.

Interactive talks

Speakers will contrast instruction following with self-initiated action. Questions from in-person and online attendees will feed into the discussion.

Hands-on tutorial

Mixed-background groups work through four scenario cards: an environmental hazard, routine maintenance, an ambiguous need for help, and a socially constrained intervention. They decide whether to act, ask, wait, or hold back.

Panel discussion

“Who Authorizes Robot Initiative?” starts from the points of agreement and disagreement collected in the tutorial and examines permission, safety, and evaluation.

Inclusive access

The CFP welcomes early, incomplete, and negative results. Recorded demonstrations can substitute for live hardware where travel, safety, or equipment constraints apply. The event will be broadcast live on Zoom.

Outreach

Publicity will use the workshop website, institutional networks, mailing lists, social media, and relevant IEEE RAS technical communities. A code of conduct and accessibility contact will be posted in advance.

After the event

With contributor consent, shareable materials will be published online. Within four weeks, an opt-in summary will collect task families, candidate metrics such as false-intervention rate and execution success, and open questions.

10 / First edition

Why a separate program?

This is a new workshop with no prior attendance history. Its half-day format concentrates on one research question and brings environmental and human-driven cases together rather than splitting them into disconnected tracks.

Scientific boundary

Not only interaction quality

For a person reaching toward a high shelf, we ask whether the observation warrants a robot-generated assistance goal, what alternatives are permissible, and whether action would create an unwanted intervention. The same logic applies to a spill or obstructed doorway with no interacting human.

Beyond execution

Not only VLA or world models

A policy can execute “clean the spill” and still fail to recognize that cleaning is needed when no instruction is issued. A world model can forecast consequences without deciding which goal merits pursuit. We prioritize goal formation before task specification, appropriate non-action, and outcome verification.
